Georgia Power Rate Plan
Which Georgia Power rate should the pro forma use?
Two paths for a dedicated EV charging site: PLM/PLL + CIEV-1 rider (demand charges phased in via BDAF, but the rider terminates once the rolling 12-month load factor exceeds 15%) or the dedicated TOU-EVC-4 EV charging schedule ($152/mo + $5.25/kW + 24.4¢ on-peak / 6.1¢ off-peak, on-peak only 2-7pm weekdays Jun-Sep). TOU-EVC-4 is typically cheaper than full-rate PLM/PLL at high volume; the CIEV rider is cheaper while it lasts. Auto (default) starts on PLM/PLL + CIEV while the load factor stays under 15%, then switches permanently to TOU-EVC-4 — operators report the transition works in that direction only (start on the rider, move to TOU later; not the reverse). Not verified in the published tariff — confirm sequencing with your Georgia Power rep before committing. TOU-EVC-4 engine is validated against a real July 2026 commercial EV charging bill (ECCR, franchise fee, and sales tax lines reproduce to the penny).
Duke Service Territory
Which Duke utility serves your site?
Duke operates separate utilities with different rate schedules. Your site's physical location determines which one applies and which rate you'll ultimately be placed on. Duke Energy Carolinas (DEC) covers Charlotte, Greensboro, Durham, Asheville. Duke Energy Progress (DEP) covers Raleigh, Wilmington, Fayetteville. DEC's demand charge is roughly half of DEP's — it matters a lot for fast-charging economics.

Price You Charge Drivers
What will you charge EV drivers per kWh?
This is the price drivers see at the pump, so to speak. In Georgia, public fast charging typically ranges from $0.42 to $0.58/kWh. Tesla's own Superchargers average around $0.52/kWh. Lower prices attract more traffic but reduce margin per session. Higher prices mean better margin but you may see reduced utilization in competitive markets. Default of $0.52/kWh is the current Tesla Supercharger national average and a fair middle-ground starting point for ROI modeling.

Financial Assumptions
Projection Period
How many years to project revenue and costs?
Tesla V4 hardware has an expected useful life of 15+ years with proper maintenance. A longer projection shows more total revenue but also introduces more uncertainty about future market conditions, EV adoption, and equipment replacement. Default of 15 years matches standard infrastructure investment analysis and the typical warranty life of charging equipment.
Discount Rate
The "time value of money" rate.
A dollar earned ten years from now is worth less to you than a dollar earned today because you could invest today's dollar and grow it. The discount rate represents your required return: if you could safely earn 10% a year investing your money elsewhere, that's your benchmark. This tool uses your discount rate to convert future cash flows into today's dollars (Net Present Value, or NPV). Default of 10% is a standard benchmark for commercial infrastructure investments. Use 8% for conservative long-term capital, 12-15% if you have higher-return alternatives available.
Utilization Growth (Yr 1, Tapers)
Starting growth rate for charging volume — it tapers automatically.
EV adoption keeps growing, but charging supply is growing with it (US DC fast ports grew ~30%/yr through 2026 while network utilization stayed flat, per Paren industry data) — new stations split your local demand. So this rate applies early and fades linearly to roughly zero by Year 15, and volume caps at a ~50% annual-average utilization ceiling (the best Tesla-owned metro site observed runs ~47%, and stalls empty out overnight with human drivers). Default of 7% starting rate is moderate for a well-sited host. Use 4-5% to be conservative, 10%+ only for underserved corridors. Autonomous vehicles charging off-peak could someday raise the ceiling — not modeled until real data exists.
First Operating Year
The calendar year your site starts selling electricity.
Used to time year-dependent modeling — chiefly the fleet charge-speed ramp: average sustained draw per vehicle rises from ~150 kW toward ~240 kW as 800V / 500 kW-class vehicles penetrate the fleet, which raises peak billing demand and the utilization ceiling in later projection years. Default of 2027 reflects a realistic install timeline for V4 equipment ordered now (equipment lead time + utility service work + permitting). Note: Georgia's oft-delayed EV charging excise tax (~$0.028/kWh, SB 146) is not modeled — see the note under the cash flow table.
Driver Sales Tax Handling
Sales tax comes out of your charging revenue.
Electricity sold to drivers is a taxable retail sale of tangible personal property (in Georgia: O.C.G.A. § 48-8-30; SB 146 § 46-2-20.1(b) expressly calls it "a sale at retail"). Networks like Tesla post tax-inclusive prices and remit the sales tax out of the gross — so a $0.52 posted price at 7% tax nets you about $0.486/kWh. Florida and South Carolina treat charging revenue as taxable retail sales as well. Default withholds the sales tax rate from the active utility panel (Sales Tax %) from all revenue. Switch to gross only if you will collect tax on top of your posted price. Sales tax is modeled as applying in all projection years — conservative, since Georgia's future motor-fuel treatment of charging could change the mix.
Sales Tax on Revenue (%)
The sales tax rate carved out of driver payments.
Separate from the sales tax on your utility bill. Real July 2026 Tesla payout statements from an Atlanta host show 6% applied against the tax-inclusive posted price (blending slightly lower, ~5.5% of gross, because free and network-internal sessions carry no tax). The same rate is also charged on the Tesla service fee itself — making the fee effectively $0.106/kWh — and both withholdings appear together in the Sales Tax column. Default of 6% matches actual Georgia payout statements. Adjust for your county or state if you have a statement showing otherwise.

Fuel Cost Recovery ($/kWh)
Georgia Power's fuel pass-through charge.
This is what Georgia Power charges to recover the cost of natural gas and coal burned to generate your electricity. It's adjusted quarterly and has ranged from $0.02 to $0.05/kWh over the past few years. Default of $0.045876/kWh matches the rate on real Georgia Power commercial invoices from August 2025.
Env. Compliance (%)
Environmental compliance cost recovery.
A percentage-based charge Georgia Power adds to cover costs of meeting state and federal environmental regulations (emissions controls, water treatment, coal ash management). Default of 13.23% reflects actual Georgia Power commercial invoices from August 2025.
Demand Side Mgmt (%)
Demand Side Management schedule.
A small line-item charge that funds Georgia Power's energy-efficiency rebate programs (the same programs that gave you the $30k EV business rebate, ironically). Default of 1.29% matches real commercial invoices. This is one of the smaller adders.
Municipal Franchise (%)
Your city's cut for letting the utility operate in their jurisdiction.
Cities charge the utility a franchise fee for using public rights-of-way, and the utility passes it through to you on your bill. Rates vary by city - Atlanta is around 4%, smaller cities may be 3% or less. Default of 3.5% is a reasonable Georgia middle-ground.
Sales Tax (%)
State + local sales tax on your electric bill.
Georgia state sales tax is 4%, plus county/city add-ons typically 3-4%. Total usually lands between 7-9% depending on jurisdiction. Default of 6% matches a real July 2026 Georgia Power commercial EV-charging invoice to the penny ($1,094.60 tax on $18,243.47). Note: buying your electricity tax-free for resale (ST-5 certificate) is legally plausible but unconfirmed — no GA DOR ruling exists for charging operators. Do not present an ST-5 to Georgia Power without a DOR letter ruling; consult a Georgia SALT professional.
Excess Facilities ($/mo)
Monthly fee for dedicated utility equipment.
If Georgia Power installs dedicated equipment that serves only your site, they may charge an ongoing monthly fee. Per guidance from Georgia Power, this charge only applies when the cost of distribution equipment exceeds a revenue ratio calculation specific to your customer account. Most sites will not have this charge. Default of $0/month reflects the typical case. Only populate this field if Georgia Power has specifically told you your site will be assessed an Excess Facilities charge.

Peak demand modeling: Tesla Model Y is used as the 2026 reference vehicle (peak ~250 kW brief, average ~150 kW sustained during the 30-minute billing demand window). As 800V / 500 kW-class vehicles penetrate the fleet, the sustained average is modeled rising ~6%/yr to a ~240 kW plateau by the mid-2030s — 500 kW itself is a fleeting peak (a Cybertruck's full V4 session still averages ~150 kW). Cabinet demand scales with how many cars charge simultaneously during peak hour, capped at 998 kW per cabinet (1,996 kW for 2-cabinet sites). Rising fleet speeds also lift the utilization kWh ceiling, at half strength — charge time shrinks but dwell time doesn't.
Fuel & Rider Adder ($/kWh)
Duke's per-kWh pass-through charges.
Bundles fuel cost recovery plus the energy-efficiency, DSM, renewable (CPRE/REPS), and securitization riders Duke layers on top of the base energy charge. In Florida this also covers the Capacity (CCR) and Environmental (ECRC) cost-recovery clauses. Default is set per selected territory. Duke files these separately from base rates and adjusts them (Florida fuel/clause charges dropped notably for 2026). Adjust if you have a recent Duke commercial invoice.
Sales Tax (%)
State + local sales tax on commercial electricity.
North Carolina applies a 7.00% combined general rate. South Carolina is ~6% state plus local option tax. Florida charges a 4.35% electricity sales tax plus county discretionary surtax. Default is set per selected territory. Adjust for your county's local add-on.
Gross Receipts Tax (%)
Florida Gross Receipts Tax on utility service.
Florida levies a 2.5% gross receipts tax on electricity, passed through on the bill. This charge does not apply in North Carolina or South Carolina (it is auto-set to 0 there). Default of 2.5% for Florida, 0% elsewhere.
Prep Credit ($ / 998 kW cabinet)
Duke Commercial Charger Prep Credit, per V4 cabinet.
Duke's make-ready credit is based on nameplate kW per charging port, which Duke measures as the cabinet's 998 kW divided by the number of ports it feeds. Because the make-ready serves the whole 998 kW cabinet, the total credit is effectively fixed per cabinet regardless of how many ports split it. This tool assumes Service Upgrades Required / New Service at a high-volume site. Default of $117,716 per cabinet (≈$117.95/kW) is calibrated to Duke's calculator: a 4-port site (249.5 kW/port) = $29,429/port × 4 = $117,716. Total credit = this value × number of cabinets (1 for the 4-post and single-cabinet 8-post builds, 2 for the 2-cabinet 8-post and 16-post builds — a key reason to spec two cabinets on an 8-post site). Covers make-ready electrical only; subject to approval, apply within 120 days of final invoice.
Peak Demand / Billing Demand
Why there is no ramp on the Duke side.
Georgia Power's CIEV-1 rider phases in demand charges over Years 1–4 (the BDAF ramp). Duke has no equivalent commercial EV rate in NC, SC, or FL — a fast-charging host pays the full demand charge from Year 1 on a standard general-service schedule. Duke schedules also carry demand ratchets (billing demand held at 50–80% of peak/contract), but for a site that peaks consistently each month, billing demand ≈ measured peak, which is how this tool models it. Peak demand uses the same Tesla Model Y reference (150 kW average per car during the 30-minute window) and concurrency scaling as the Georgia Power side.
